A few weeks ago, my PhD advisor, [Ryan Corey](https://www.ryan-corey.com/), mentioned that his advisor's advisor was connected to *Bose*. I was curious whether he meant the audio company. It turned out to be **Amar Bose**, the MIT professor who founded it.

That conversation sent me down an unexpected rabbit hole: who trained my advisors, who trained *their* advisors, and how far back could I trace those connections?

I have two routes to explore. I am doing my PhD at the University of Illinois Chicago with Ryan Corey, and I previously completed an MS by Research at IIT Mandi with Padmanabhan Rajan. Both routes lead into the history of signal processing, though in different ways.

## My PhD lineage: UIC to MIT

Here is the advisor chain I found, beginning with me and moving backward:

**Rajesh Rameshbabu** (UIC)  
↓ **Ryan M. Corey** (my PhD advisor)  
↓ **Andrew C. Singer**  
↓ **Alan V. Oppenheim**  
↓ **Amar G. Bose**  
↓ **Yuk-Wing Lee**  
↓ **Norbert Wiener**

There are several distinctions worth making. A line in this tree represents doctoral supervision as recorded in academic sources; it does not imply that each person's research was simply an extension of their advisor's. For example, Bose was intellectually close to Wiener, but the MIT thesis record identifies **Yuk-Wing Lee** as Bose's formal doctoral advisor. That is why Lee appears between them.

What surprised me was the sequence of research areas. Wiener helped establish the mathematics of stochastic processes, filtering, and cybernetics. Lee worked in statistical communication theory. Bose studied acoustics and nonlinear systems. Oppenheim helped shape digital signal processing. Singer worked in signal processing and communications, and Corey works on microphone arrays and audio systems.

My own research is on multichannel audio, source separation, microphone bleed reduction, and acoustic sensing. There is a recognizable history of signals and systems here, even though the specific problems and methods have changed enormously.

The trail may continue backward from Wiener through **Karl Schmidt**, and potentially into nineteenth-century philosophy. I am leaving those older connections out of the main diagram until I can support every supervisory link with equally strong records.

## My master's lineage: IIT Mandi to room acoustics

The other branch begins with my MS by Research at IIT Mandi:

**Rajesh Rameshbabu** (IIT Mandi)  
↓ **Padmanabhan Rajan** (my MS research advisor)  
↓ **Hema A. Murthy**  
↓ **Bayya Yegnanarayana**  
↓ **B. S. Ramakrishna**

**A note about terminology:** My link to Rajan is a *master's research-supervision relationship*, not a doctoral advisor–student relationship. The remaining arrows trace doctoral supervision. I call this my MS *mentorship lineage* rather than presenting the entire chain as a strict PhD genealogy.

This route is particularly interesting to me because of its connection to physical acoustics. Yegnanarayana's doctoral research addressed nonexponential sound decay in rooms. He later became well known for speech analysis and representations that extract information from phase and excitation signals. Murthy's work also spans speech, music, and phase-based analysis. Rajan's research brought me into musical audio interference reduction.

I did not know this history when I started working on microphone bleed. Discovering it afterward does not make my research inherited or inevitable. But it is fascinating that questions about rooms, speech, and audio representations recur across this branch.

Ramakrishna earned his doctorate in acoustics from Illinois Institute of Technology in 1949. I have not found a primary record naming his PhD advisor, so **this is where I stop the verified MS-side tree**. It is also a funny geographical coincidence: that older branch passed through Chicago long before I came here for my PhD.

## And then there is the Erdős number

An **Erdős number** is the shortest number of coauthorship steps separating a researcher from the mathematician [Paul Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erd%C5%91s).

Erdős has number 0. A direct coauthor has number 1; a coauthor of one of those researchers has number at most 2; and so on. The distance is measured using *joint publications*, not mentorship or citations.

I first became interested in calculating mine because I have coauthored papers with Corey, and a third-party coauthorship database lists **Corey's Erdős number as 3**. If that database path is correct and uses the same authors, this would put my own number at **no more than 4**.

That is a **tentative upper bound, not a verified exact Erdős number**. I have not independently checked every publication in the path or ruled out shorter routes through my other coauthors. Until I can do that, I would rather write **Erdős number: ≤ 4 (provisional)** than announce a precise result.

The distinction is actually part of the fun: an academic family tree, a coauthorship graph, and a citation network are three different ways of connecting scientists.

## What I take from these connections

The part I enjoy most is not being a few names away from well-known researchers. Academic ancestry is not a measure of research quality, and it says nothing about whether my own results will hold up.

What I like is seeing how research traditions travel. The UIC route runs through statistical communication and DSP. The IIT Mandi route runs through acoustics and speech processing. Both eventually intersect the questions I work on today: how to represent sound, understand propagation, and separate signals that overlap.

A conversation about Bose turned into a small personal history project. I am glad I followed the curiosity.
